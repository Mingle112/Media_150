Writing Reflections

1. In Plato's Cave — Susan Sontag

There is a childhood game that I always internally revisit when I am taking a photo: the telephone game, where one person speaks to one person and passes it along to another and another. It's interesting because the answer or message passed along changes every time and through every person. Sontag, writing in "In Plato's Cave," reminds me of when she wrote , "Precisely by slicing out this moment and freezing it, all photographs testify to time's relentless melt." But not merely agreeing, I do believe a photograph freezes a moment to testify to time's relentless melt. However, the moment freezes, but our interpretation of it changes throughout our personal growth.

Just recently, I sat with myself reminiscing the photos I took 2 years ago from my trip to my hometown in China, and it was just a few: 15 images across 1 month of time, the moments that I thought were worth the most to record, or at least visually pleasing to me to capture. And now I sit in regret. Why didn't I take more photos? Very ridiculous. And so I realized the image that we absorb in the present is sometimes different from what we saw in the past. It means something different to every stage of us. Two years ago, I didn't care so much about taking pictures because I was a true believer of "capturing down" the moment only through my own eyes and who I am in the moment, letting myself indulge in the moment and forget about the future. But now, not being able to teleport myself immediately to the moment makes me sad because I can't relive that happiness again.

But that also makes me wonder: why am I having such a big reaction or even grieving about not being able to clear up the blurriness of that memory in my head? Would I still have that feeling and longing of that moment, trying to grab tight onto that feeling again, if that memory is clear? Would I be too satisfied to simply forget about it after seeing that clear and precise photo of that moment again, the precise and exact image that I can access immediately when I want to? Do I want to cherish that moment or for it to simply be like any impulse purchases at 3 a.m. night on Amazon, and lose interest right after I unwrap my package? Sometimes I think letting the memory/moment be vague is better than recounting every delicate detail of it.

Our world at this moment is my world. Everyone's experiences create their individual understanding of the world, so I can only describe my own world. If I can use any sort of visual media to represent our world at this moment, it will be an illustration. Every stroke does not have to be defined, but instead it is blurry and vague. Every blurry memory fragment combines to be something sharp and clear. Photography is quick and sharp; it comes in a snap. But an illustration of my world at this moment requires many strokes, and many of them are blurry, and I need time to piece them together.

2. Your Brain on ChatGPT

After reading Your Brain on ChatGPT and watching Brain Experts WARNING: Watch This Before Using ChatGPT Again!, one thing I kept thinking about was why people use LLMs in the first place. A lot of it comes down to saving time. If an LLM allows us to finish something faster, why can't we use that time for something we actually want to do or learn? And is learning strictly measured by whether we can write an essay completely on our own? I don't think using AI has to mean giving the thinking over to it. An LLM can also become something like a personal teacher. I can ask why my answer is wrong, have it explain something differently when I don't understand, or let it guide me through a problem while I still work toward the answer myself.

I also don't think one study can show every way people use AI. The MIT study focused specifically on essay writing, and the researchers themselves point out limitations in the participants and whether the results can be generalized to other LLMs or other tasks. I wonder how the results might differ for people with learning or cognitive disabilities who use AI as support. Asking an LLM to teach you something is very different from asking it to complete the work for you.

But the video made me return to my first question differently. What exactly are we saving time from? I first saw difficulty as something AI could help us get through quicker. Then I started thinking about the difficulty itself. If I struggle with something, get it wrong, return to it, and finally understand it, isn't that struggle part of learning? The discussion in the video about keeping our brains active, including its discussion around Dementia and Alzheimer's, also made me think more about the value of continuing to challenge our brains. At the same time, it made me question whether making everything easier should always be the goal.

And then there is another question. Why are we so concerned with saving time in the first place? As for me, a current college student, I think part of that pressure comes from how many things we are expected to do at the same time. There are classes, but around them there are also internships, clubs, research labs, work, and the thought of what happens after graduation. These are choices that I make, but they are also things we are told will help us prepare for the future. If the standard keeps moving toward doing more within the same amount of time, choosing the slower way can start to feel like falling behind. Maybe our dependence on AI is also connected to why being slow has become something we are afraid of.

For me, balance is somewhere between refusing AI and handing everything over to it. One idea from the Arts & AI panel event today stayed with me. We should still use our brains for the creative things that actually matter to us. If I write a letter to someone I love, I want my words in it. If I create a song, I want my own effort in it, even when it is imperfect. However, AI can help me with the "fake things," like finding an error in my code or quickly finding the mistakes in my music tuning. I want AI to help me return to my own thinking, rather than give me a reason to leave it.

3. Motherhood in Crisis

The chapter that I chose was Chapter 3, "The Devil at My Door," which illustrates the story of a woman in Sierra Leone who lost her daughter and other young children due to the poorly structured and underfunded healthcare system. In many cases, the woman turned to a traditional healer in her village, but instead of receiving proper medical treatment, all she heard were myths about demon possession and witchcraft. After reading this chapter, I realized that one of the reasons illustrations were used instead of real-life photographs was that they allow a more sensitive approach to telling these women's stories. The hardships that these women in developing countries are enduring are already cruel enough. Due to the lack of proper healthcare, many are unable to receive the treatment they need, and in many cases, they also face stigmatized judgments. Instead of using a series of real-life photographs to showcase these experiences, illustrations allow the women in these stories to maintain more dignity. For example, in this chapter, there was a scene where the mother was deeply in pain while being examined at the hospital. Scenes like this can be especially sensitive for women who are already suffering, and I think it would be inhumane to point a camera at the mother while she is going through such a difficult experience. At the same time, illustrations allow for more creative freedom. Near the beginning of the chapter, there was a scene where the mother was grieving, with her face buried in her palms. The artist added hatched lines around her head to demonstrate her frustration and depression as she experienced mistreatment. Unlike real-life photographs, which capture what is physically happening, illustrations can exaggerate certain expressions and details to help readers understand what the characters are feeling. Something like this would be difficult to achieve through photography alone. After analyzing the chapter, I also realized that using illustrations in a graphic novel style, rather than only text or photography, allows the story to reach a younger adult audience. Many younger adults may be more attracted to illustrated stories than pure text or real-life photographs, which require readers to piece together the story themselves using the limited information provided. However, the choice to include real-life footage and photographs of the actual people at the end creates a different effect. It leaves a deeper impression on readers by reminding them that the graphic novel they just read is based on real experiences, and that these struggles are still happening in places we don't often see. I think this combination of illustrations and real-life footage makes the story more impactful while also raising awareness about the need to improve healthcare in developing countries where medical resources and technology are still far behind.
